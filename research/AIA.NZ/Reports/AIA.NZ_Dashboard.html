DCF Valuation

⚠ Model approach & DCF sanity-check result: This is a component-built owner-FCF DCF, not a simple multiple-of-FCF extrapolation. Reported free cash flow has been negative every year FY2023-FY2025 (-NZ$247m to -NZ$581m) and management guides a further NZ$1.0-1.6bn/yr of capex through roughly FY2029/30 as the domestic jet terminal and airfield expansion are built. Owner FCF is built bottom-up from EBITDAFI margin, D&A, tax, management's own capex guidance and working capital over a 10-year explicit horizon — long enough to carry this debt-funded capex supercycle through its peak (~FY2028) and its taper toward a ~NZ$320m/yr maintenance level — discounted at a 7.5% WACC with a terminal value capped at the lower of Gordon growth or a 20x FCF multiple. Result: a base intrinsic value of NZ$1.90 vs a market price of NZ$8.76 (-78% downside) — implying just ~6.2x EV/EBITDAFI against AIA's own 17.8x historical average (ex-outlier years) and today's 24.6x market multiple. The built-in DCF sanity check passed, but in the opposite direction to the one it is designed to catch: it flags the DCF as cheap versus the market, not expensive. That gap is read as the market paying a duration/scarcity premium for irreplaceable monopoly airport infrastructure that a textbook Gordon-growth DCF, discounting years of capex-supercycle cash burn, structurally cannot reproduce — not as evidence the stock is dramatically overvalued. The bull case reaches NZ$3.84 and the probability-weighted IV (25% Bull / 50% Base / 25% Bear) is NZ$1.74. The bear case's negative equity value (-NZ$0.68/share) and the base case's negative 15%-CAGR entry price (−NZ$0.06) are leverage-risk warning signals, not literal price targets — most of the capex programme is already contractually committed (NZ$1.31bn of PP&E purchase obligations at Dec-2025) and debt-funded, so it does not simply switch off in a downside scenario. FY2026 annual results (due 20-Aug-2026) are not yet reflected in this model. Treat the DCF outputs below as a conservative cash-flow floor and cross-check against the EV/EBITDAFI memo and the regulated-asset-base (RAB)/book-value memo further down this section, not as a standalone fair value call.

The DCF's base intrinsic value of NZ$1.90 implies an EV of only ~6.2x FY2025 EBITDAFI — far below both the current 24.6x market multiple and AIA's own 17.8x historical trading average. Applying today's 24.6x multiple to Year-10 base-case EBITDAFI (NZ$1,252m) implies an EV of roughly NZ$30.8bn, multiples of the DCF-derived enterprise value. This is not a DCF error: it quantifies the size of the duration/scarcity premium the market assigns to irreplaceable, regulated monopoly airport infrastructure — a premium a textbook WACC-based Gordon-growth DCF, discounting a multi-year capex-driven cash burn, structurally cannot reproduce. Note the underlying EV/EBITDAFI inputs are FY2025-based; FY2026 annual results (due 20-Aug-2026) may shift both the EBITDAFI base and the multiple once released.

AIA does not publish a single consolidated regulated asset base (RAB) figure the way a pure price-cap utility would — its dual-till model mixes a Commerce Commission-monitored aeronautical asset base with wholly unregulated retail/property/car-park assets — so a precise RAB-multiple cross-check cannot be built from public disclosure alone. As a proxy, book shareholders' equity (NZ$10,582.3m at H1-2026, reflecting substantial capitalised capex already spent on the terminal/airfield programme plus historical investment-property revaluations) provides a floor-level anchor: the current market cap of ~NZ$14,943.9m (1,705.7m shares × NZ$8.76) implies a P/B of ~1.41x — within the historical 1.25x–2.11x (ex-outlier) trading range. That premium to book is far smaller than the DCF-implied gap (market cap vs base-case DCF equity value of NZ$3,234.1m, a ~4.6x multiple) because book value already capitalises the committed capex spend that the DCF has not yet turned into cash flow — i.e. the market is paying close to book for hard infrastructure assets plus a modest premium for the regulated monopoly franchise, a much smaller and more defensible gap than the pure discounted-owner-cash-flow gap. This is a sanity check, not a valuation method in its own right: it reinforces that the DCF gap is a duration/terminal-value modelling artefact of the capex supercycle rather than evidence AIA trades at an indefensible multiple of its underlying regulated and physical asset base.
