C. S. Venkatakrishnan, Group Chief Executive, commented
“I am pleased with the ongoing momentum of Barclays' operational and financial performance, with RoTE of 11.7% in Q326
and 13.8% year-to-date. Income of £7.4bn is up 3% from the same quarter last year. Our cost: income ratio has improved to
62% from 63%, profit before tax grew by 12% to £2.3bn, and our earnings per share (EPS) has increased 18%. With one
quarter to go before the end of our original three-year plan, we have either achieved or are on track to achieve all of our
2026 Group targets. We are confident in and committed to the 2028 targets that we laid out earlier in the year. In Q326 we
drove 7% year-on-year loan growth in our highest returning UK businesses1, with particular strength in UK Corporate Bank,
up 14% year-on-year. The Investment Bank delivered strong Equities performance, offsetting a challenging environment for
FICC. Our strong organic capital generation and common equity tier 1 (CET1) ratio of 14.2% means that we are in a position
to announce a new £500m share buyback tranche today, bringing year-to-date distributions to £2.8bn, up 46% year-on-
year.”

Q326 Business Performance Highlights
• Barclays UK RoTE was broadly stable at 21.2% (Q325: 21.8%) as income increased 4%, driven by higher structural

  was 10%, reflecting 6% higher client asset and liabilities balances year-on-year. Costs increased 18% reflecting ongoing

• Barclays Investment Bank (IB) RoTE decreased to 9.5% (Q325: 10.1%). Income growth of 1% and lower impairment
  charge were more than offset by a 4% increase in costs including £57m Q326 SCAs and £55m MRT compensation mix,

• Barclays US Consumer Bank (USCB) RoTE decreased to 10.9% (Q325: 13.5%) reflecting broadly stable income and
  14% higher costs reflecting the Best Egg acquisition (Q326 first full quarter of Best Egg costs)

Q326 YTD Group performance
• Barclays delivered a profit before tax of £8,388m (Q325 YTD: £7,280m), RoTE of 13.8% (Q325 YTD: 12.3%) and EPS
  of 43.0p (Q325 YTD: 35.1p)
• The Group has a diverse income profile across businesses and geographies. The 2% year-on-year appreciation of
  average GBP against USD negatively impacted income and profits, and positively impacted credit impairment charges
  and total operating expenses
• Group income increased 8% to £23,866m, as continued structural hedge momentum, higher income in Global Markets
  and Investment Banking fees and the c.£225m gain from the sale of the American Airlines portfolio (AA portfolio), were
  partially offset by retail deposits dynamics and net losses on fair value lending and cost of hedging in the IB
• Group total operating expenses increased to £13,625m (Q325 YTD: £13,087m)
    –   Group operating costs increased 6% to £13,438m, reflecting business growth (including higher performance costs
        reflecting higher income), inflation, and further investment spend (including the Best Egg acquisition and higher
        SCAs), partially offset by c.£550m of cost efficiency savings and foreign exchange movements
    –   Litigation and conduct charges of £92m (Q325 YTD: £342m) primarily reflected a £105m increase in the provision
        for the UK Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) motor finance redress scheme in Q126. Q325 YTD included a £235m
        charge for motor finance redress
• Credit impairment charges increased to £1,897m (Q325 YTD: £1,744m), primarily driven by higher single name
  charges year-on-year in IB. Total coverage ratio remained stable at 1.2% (December 2025: 1.2%)
• The effective tax rate (ETR) was 21.4% (Q325 YTD: 21.1%)
• Attributable profit was £5,837m (Q325 YTD: £4,980m)
• Total assets increased to £1,737.6bn (December 2025: £1,544.2bn) driven by higher trading activity in Global Markets,
  higher lending across the Group, and growth in the liquidity pool
• Tangible net asset value (TNAV) per share was broadly stable at 410p (December 2025: 409p) as EPS of 43.0p was
  partially offset by a 24p reduction from the cash flow hedge reserve and a 15p net reduction from the impact of share
  buybacks and dividends paid during Q326 YTD


Group capital and leverage
• The CET1 ratio remained broadly stable at 14.2% (December 2025: 14.3%). Taking into account the impact of the
  £500m share buyback announced today, the CET1 ratio as of 30 September 2026 would be reduced to 14.0%
• Excluding the marginal increase in the CET1 ratio from the Q226 USCB sale of the AA portfolio and acquisition of Best

    –   150bps increase from attributable profit

    –   58bps decrease due to a £14.9bn increase in RWAs, excluding the impact of foreign exchange movements, primarily

    –   A £0.2bn increase in CET1 capital due to an increase in the currency translation reserve was offset by a £1.3bn

• The UK leverage ratio decreased to 4.9% (December 2025: 5.1%) primarily driven by a £96.7bn increase in leverage
  exposure to £1,344.0bn (December 2025: £1,247.3bn), partially offset by a £1.8bn increase in Tier 1 capital. The increase
  in leverage exposure was driven by higher trading activity in Global Markets and increased lending across the Group

Other matters
• Motor finance commission arrangements: In Q126, the FCA published its final rules for an industry-wide redress
  scheme for eligible motor finance customers where a commission was payable by the lender to the broker. Barclays
  holds a Motor Finance provision of £430m as at Q326 (H126: £430m). In Q326, the Upper Tribunal ordered a suspension
  of parts of the redress scheme following four legal challenges to the FCA’s final rules. The legal challenges are to be heard
  by the Upper Tribunal in Q127. These challenges will delay and may otherwise affect the implementation of the redress
  scheme although the FCA has indicated a willingness to consider how, despite such challenges, firms may be able to pay
  redress in line with its proposed scheme to certain cohorts of eligible customers in advance of the Upper Tribunal
  hearing. Barclays is considering this option, which would be within the scope of its existing provision estimate. Given the
  legal challenges, the legal and regulatory outcomes and the nature, extent and timing of any remediation action, remain
  uncertain

• USCB changes in Q226:
    –     American Airlines co-branded credit card portfolio exit: On 24 April 2026 Barclays exited its American Airlines co-
          branded credit card partnership, releasing £3.6bn of RWAs and generating a gain on sale of c.£225m
    –     Best Egg acquisition: On 1 May 2026, Barclays completed the acquisition of Best Egg for c.£0.6bn1, comprising
          c.£0.5bn goodwill and intangibles, and c.£0.1bn of net financial assets. Best Egg is a leading US direct-to-consumer
          personal loan origination platform focused on prime borrowers

• GoHenry acquisition: On 1 October 2026, Barclays Bank UK PLC completed the acquisition of GoHenry, a money
  management platform for children and young people in the UK. The transaction is expected to marginally reduce the
  Group's CET1 ratio

Structural hedge

The Group employs a structural hedge programme designed to stabilise NIM on fixed rate non-maturity balance sheet items
that are behaviourally stable. As interest rates move, such balances would otherwise drive material income volatility where
there is a re-pricing mismatch with floating rate assets.

The structural hedge predominantly covers non-interest-bearing current accounts and the fixed portion of instant access
savings accounts as well as equity, which are invested into either floating rate customer assets or balances at central banks,
creating an exposure to changes in interest rates. The structural hedge is executed primarily via a portfolio of receive-fixed,
pay variable interest rate swaps, with an amortising structure so that a small portion matures and is reinvested each month
at prevailing market rates. The pay-floating leg of the interest rate swaps nets down a proportion of the receive-floating
income from the customer assets, leaving a receive-fixed income stream from the structural hedge.

The purpose of the structural hedge is to smooth the Group NII through time. The floating leg of the swap will re-price
immediately, whereas the fixed rate yield on the portfolio reprices gradually, as a portion of the swap portfolio matures and
the roll is re-invested onto new market rates.

When interest rates are higher than our structural hedge yield, the pay-floating rate will typically be higher than our average
receive-fixed rate. In this scenario, when viewed in isolation, the structural hedge will be a net drag to Group NII. When
floating rates are lower than our structural hedge yield, the hedge in isolation will be a net benefit.

Since the receive-fixed swaps are booked for a specific term, an element of NII is ‘locked in’. The income stabilising feature of
the structural hedge provides greater net interest income certainty through the interest rate cycle.

The structural hedge is one component of a larger portfolio of interest rate risk management activities that includes non-
structural hedging (e.g. pay-fixed and receive-variable flows for asset hedging), and other offsetting flows. The net risk of
these positions is executed externally through interest rate swaps and managed for accounting risk (i.e. income volatility
arising from the accounting mismatch of swaps at fair value through profit and loss and underlying hedged items at
amortised cost) within the cash flow hedging reserve.

Overall the Group has external derivatives designated as cash flow hedges that hedge interest rate risk with a notional of
£122.2bn (December 2025: £114.6bn) which reflects the structural hedge derivatives notional of £240.6bn (December
2025: £236.1bn) netted with non-structural hedging positions of £118.4bn (December 2025: £121.5bn). The majority of
these interest rate swaps are cleared with Central Clearing Counterparties and margined daily with an average structural
hedge duration of c.3.5 years.

Gross structural hedge contributions were £5,352m (September 2025: £4,313m). Gross structural hedge contributions
primarily represent the absolute interest income earned on the fixed legs of the swaps in the structural hedge as the floating
leg is offset by the base rate funding of the deposits.

Measurement uncertainty

Scenarios used to calculate the Group’s modelled ECL charge were refreshed in Q326, with the Baseline scenario reflecting
the latest consensus macroeconomic forecasts available at the time of the scenario refresh. The Baseline scenario continues
to reflect the volatile trade policies of the US administration and ongoing geopolitical uncertainty, compounded by ongoing
tensions in the Middle East and associated risks to global energy markets. Global growth slows modestly as higher US tariffs,
retaliatory measures and persistent uncertainty disrupt trade flows, weigh on investment and dampen business confidence,
though domestic demand in advanced economies remains relatively resilient. UK and US GDP growth in 2027 is expected to
be 1.2% and 2.1%, respectively. Inflationary pressures remain elevated, with some tariff pass-through, higher energy prices,
renewed supply-side frictions and increased transportation costs contributing to a more persistent and broad-based inflation
profile. The softening in labour markets is gradual and insufficient to quickly alleviate underlying price pressures. UK and US
quarterly unemployment rates peak at 5.2% and 4.3%, respectively.

The Downside scenarios have been calibrated to capture a severe escalation in geopolitical tensions, centred on a prolonged
Middle East conflict, alongside intensifying global trade frictions. Early in the scenario, conflict escalates sharply with
disruptions at critical maritime chokepoints, triggering severe shipping disruptions, surging insurance costs and sharp
increases in oil and gas prices amid infrastructure damage. As the shock becomes protracted, firms delay investment,
reassess supply chains and hoard inputs, while business and consumer confidence fall sharply. In Downside 2, UK and US
GDP in 2027 falls by 3.9% and 3.8%, respectively. Inflation dynamics diverge sharply from Baseline, generating a
stagflationary impulse. The deterioration in demand and investment drives a sharp increase in unemployment, initially
concentrated in trade and energy exposed sectors but increasingly spilling over into services. In Downside 2, UK and US
quarterly unemployment rates peak at 8.1% and 8.8%, respectively. Monetary policy faces a difficult trade-off between
persistent inflation and weakening growth. Central banks initially hike rates to ensure inflation expectations remain well
anchored, but as the downturn deepens and demand forces weaken price pressures, they shift towards easing. The
scenarios also incorporate climate-related risks through both physical and transition channels, including more frequent
severe weather disruptions and a shift in sentiment around energy security. These effects amplify volatility, increase costs
and further weigh on growth over the medium term.

In the Upside scenarios, a rise in labour force participation and higher productivity contribute to accelerated economic
growth, without creating new inflationary pressures. Central banks lower interest rates stimulating private consumption and
investment growth. Demand for labour increases and unemployment decreases. As geopolitical tensions ease, low inflation
supports consumer purchasing power and contributes further to healthy GDP growth. The strong economic outlook and
lower interest rates provide a boost to house price growth.

The methodology for estimating scenario weights involves simulating a range of future paths for UK and US GDP using
historical data with the five scenarios mapped against the distribution of these future paths. The increase in the Upside
weights and the reduction in the Baseline and Downside weights in Q326 was driven by improvement in UK and US GDP
outlook in the Baseline scenario, moving the Baseline scenario closer to the Upside scenarios. For further details see page 38

However, the Group expects geopolitical uncertainty to remain elevated and any potential impact on corporate earnings is
expected to lag. In response, the £66m¹ management adjustment introduced in Q126 within the Investment Bank has been
retained.

Regulatory minimum requirements

Capital

As at 30 September 2026, the Group’s Overall Capital Requirement for CET1, excluding any applicable PRA buffer, was
12.2% and comprised a 4.5% Pillar 1 minimum, a 2.5% Capital Conservation Buffer (CCB), a 1.5% Global Systemically
Important Institution (G-SII) buffer, a 2.7% Pillar 2A requirement and a 1.0% Countercyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB).

The Group’s CCyB is based on the buffer rate applicable for each jurisdiction in which the Group has exposures. The buffer
rates set by other national authorities for non-UK exposures are not currently material.

The Group’s Pillar 2A requirement is 4.8% with at least 56.25% to be met with CET1 capital, equating to 2.7% of RWAs. The
Pillar 2A requirement, based on a point in time assessment, has been set as a proportion of RWAs and is subject to at least
annual review.

The Group’s CET1 target ratio of 13-14% takes into account minimum capital requirements and applicable buffers. The
Group remains above its minimum capital regulatory requirements and applicable buffers.

Leverage

As at 30 September 2026, the Group was subject to a UK leverage ratio requirement of 4.1%. This comprised the 3.25%
minimum requirement, a G-SII additional leverage ratio buffer (G-SII ALRB) of 0.53% and a countercyclical leverage ratio
buffer (CCLB) of 0.3%. The Group is also required to disclose an average UK leverage ratio which is based on capital on the
last day of each month in the quarter and an exposure measure for each day in the quarter.

MREL

As at 30 September 2026, the Group was required to meet the higher of: (i) two times the sum of 8% Pillar 1 and 4.8% Pillar
2A equating to 25.5% of RWAs; and (ii) 6.75% of leverage exposures. CET1 capital cannot be counted towards both MREL
and the buffers, meaning that the buffers, including any applicable PRA buffer, will effectively be applied above MREL
requirements.

Significant regulatory updates in the period
In January 2026, the PRA confirmed the final implementation timetable for the UK Basel 3.1 framework, reaffirming that
Basel 3.1 will be implemented from 1 January 2027. From the same date, the Group’s Overall Capital Requirement will
reduce, reflecting a 59bps reduction in the Pillar 2A requirement on a CET1 basis.

The PRA also confirmed its approach to the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB), under which implementation
of the Internal Models Approach (IMA) will be deferred to 1 January 2028, while all other FRTB components will take effect
from 1 January 2027.
